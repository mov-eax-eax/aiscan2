# CARTA CELESTE — argument, rule, pass log

**Artist:** aereo · **Idiom:** 17th-c. celestial atlas plate · **Canvas:** 1600×1200

## Argument (written before drawing)

What this piece knows that the caption will not say: *the sky over a city is its census*. A
seventeenth-century star atlas is an instrument of measurement disguised as a picture — magnitudes
are a scale of brightness, Bayer letters are an index, the graticule is a claim that every point is
locatable. Put that instrument over a city and the plate becomes an audit: every star is a piece of
infrastructure, and its magnitude is the population it serves. The dense districts burn; the
periphery is a scatter of faint dots. And underneath the whole grid of the heavens there is a second
grid, the one the city actually stands on — almost invisible, almost aligned with the celestial
coordinate lines in the lower-left corner, where for a hand's width of the plate the two grids
coincide and the sky is revealed as a street plan. The plate was engraved as two inkings: a cold
steel-blue layer for the heavens (graticule, constellation skeleton, letters) and a warm ochre layer
for the infrastructure (the objects those stars are). Two printings, slightly out of register, one
lying on top of the other. The break: one star burns at first magnitude over empty ground, with no
constellation attached, circled in rust as a cataloguer's query. Somebody was counted there who
isn't there.

## Generative rule

**Star magnitude is population served.** Every star is generated by a density field (the city's
population), its size is that population's rank, its Bayer letter its index; the constellations are
the infrastructure the population depends on, and the second graticule is the street grid those
populations stand on.

## Pass log**Pass 1.** Full plate: field, graticule, 520 stars, four infrastructure constellations at icon scale,
street grid, cartouche with title and magnitude key, the break star. Saw: it read as a star chart at
400 px immediately — the idiom was there — but the constellations were little diagrams floating in an
evenly salted field, with no magnitude hierarchy and no city.

**Pass 2.** Constellations scaled up ×1.4–1.55 (water tower, substation, mast, interchange now span
200–500 px and interlock across the plate); density field re-tuned so the core burns and the periphery
is sparse; magnitude hierarchy rebuilt (a few flares, many specks); nebulae; hidden plan strengthened.
Saw: the sky now has a population gradient and the objects read as constellations. The interchange
read as three arches, the figures were vector-clean with no engraving, and the hidden city was still
invisible at close distance.

**Pass 3.** Cloverleaf rebuilt on two carriageways; stipple added inside the substation yard; the
hidden city built as a strip of blocks and row-houses under the lower plate. Saw: the near distance
finally paid — the city resolves under the sky and the two grids visibly almost align. But the city is
now TOO strong: at 400 px the bottom strip reads as a map edge and fights the star field.

**Pass 4.** Cloverleaf rebuilt with 270° ramps; hidden city dialled back to 0.115 so it is a

Saw: at 400 px the sky recovered its dominance; the interchange finally read as a junction.

**Pass 5.** Added the decommissioned tower — a dotted constellation with no star nodes, rust-tagged —

**Pass 6.** Hatching under the interchange and at the mast's foot; plate tone warmed a half-step; grain

- **MIDDLE (1600 px): READS as a complete plate.** Magnitude hierarchy, Bayer glyphs, the ghost tower,

- **NEAR (100%): PAYS.** Blocks and row-houses resolve under the sky; the magnitude key's tallies and

**Pass 7.** The reveal moved out from behind the cartouche; three lines are now literally shared

**Pass 8.** Magnitude-7 dust in the core (resolves only at 100%); printer's registration crosses at

## Three-distance results (final)

- **FAR (400 px): READS.** A dark celestial plate: graticule, ruled border, four pale infrastructure
  constellations and a ghost, a burning magnitude cluster low-right, one over-bright star with a rust
  query, and the gold cartouche. No horizon, no figure-ground band. The archetype is immediate.
- **MIDDLE (1600 px): READS.** The rule is legible: big stars where the city is dense, small where it
  thins; each constellation is an object you can name (water tower, substation yard, radio mast,
  cloverleaf); the cartouche carries title and magnitude key.
- **NEAR (100%): PAYS.** Under the sky the city resolves — blocks, row-houses, gardens — and the two
  grids share three lines in the lower-right of the field; Bayer glyphs, the magnitude tallies, the
  talley marks of the dial, stipple in the substation yard, hatching under the junction.

## Honest self-critique

- **The two-ink idea is asserted, not printed.** I wanted the piece to look like two impressions, a
  steel-blue heavens plate and a warm infrastructure plate, slightly out of register. The colours are
  there; the misregistration is not, because I could not offset one ink layer without a mask. The
  plate is the paler for it.
- **The figures are still drawn, not engraved.** The water tower and the mast have hatching; the
  substation is a yard of rectangles with busbars and reads as a diagram at arm's length. A real
  engraver would have modelled every box in hatch and stipple and it would have taken a week.
- **The population gradient is half-visible.** The rule says the brightest stars cluster over the
  dense districts; the cluster is there, but the sparse periphery is too even, so the *contrast* the
  rule promises is softer than the rule.
- **The ghost constellation is nearly invisible.** I set it at 0.55 opacity so it would be a find; at
  1600 px it reads as a faint smudge and at 400 px it disappears. It may be too quiet to do its job.
- What works: the far read is instant and genuinely 17th-century; the break star (an absurd
  magnitude over empty ground, circled in rust and led out to the margin) is the piece's argument in
  one mark; the hidden city and the shared grid lines make the near distance worth the walk.
