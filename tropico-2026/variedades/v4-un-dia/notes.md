# UN DÍA — working notes

**Artist:** aereo · **Style:** monochrome photographic contact sheet · **Canvas:** 1600×1200
**Archetype:** 4×6 contact-sheet grid. The grid IS the composition.
**Subject:** one day on one city block. A fixed camera at a corner; a wall, a doorway, a window, a
kerb and a lamp post; twenty-four exposures between 05:40 and 22:50.

**Concept.** The sheet is the picture. Each frame is a fragment of the same wall and would be
nothing on its own — a grey rectangle with a dark door in it — but the roll is a clock: dawn comes
up on the wall, the market sets itself up under an awning, noon burns the wall white and drags a
shadow band across it, a dog crosses the pavement, rain arrives, the window lights, the block goes
black. One frame of the twenty-four is *unexposed*: it prints black. That void is the only thing
the photographer marked, with the sheet's single red frame number.

**Organising rule.** One camera, one lens, one viewpoint. Nothing is reframed except what the light
does and what crosses the pavement. The row seams (cornice at y=64, kerb at y=108 in frame
coordinates) line up across every gutter, so each row reads as one pan cut into four cells, and the
grid refuses to become twenty-four small pictures. Value is the clock: the sheet runs dark (dawn) →
mid (market) → blown (noon) → mid-dark (dog) → grey (rain) → black (night).

**Palette.** Photographic paper, carbon black, and a full monochrome ramp — with one exception, the
red frame number (the only chroma on the sheet). Frame numbers are ticks, not digits: only V2 in
this room may use letterforms.
## Pass log

**Pass 1.** 24 frames, 4×6, film-edge strips top and bottom, frame-number ticks, sheet marks. Saw:
the paper was as dark as the frames, so the whole sheet read as one grey slab; every frame had the
same value; the "dawn" row was lighter than the "market" row, so there was no clock; the noon
shadows were not believable; the red tick was invisible.

**Pass 2.** Built the value ladder: dawn dark, market mid, noon blown, afternoon mid-dark, rain
grey, night near-black. Ground separated from wall, kerb shadow added, hard noon shadow,
soffit at the top of each frame, grid shifted off-centre (300/347 left-right), paper lightened,
stains reduced. Saw: better clock, but the frames were suspiciously identical — a vector stage set.

**Pass 3.** Added per-frame exposure drift (each exposure a little over or under), camera jitter of
±1.3 px, a per-frame vignette and sweep light, heavier grain. Removed the blur from the noon
shadows (noon light is hard). Saw: the roll started to feel hand-held; one noon frame still had a
huge black wedge that read as a graphic shape rather than a shadow.

**Pass 4.** Replaced that wedge with a narrow diagonal shadow band; rebuilt the rain figure as a
person under an umbrella (it had read as a mushroom on a post); red frame number enlarged; paper
stain softened. Converged.

## Honest self-critique

- **The block is implied, not shown.** A wall, a door, a window and a kerb are a *façade*, not a
  street: there is no second plane and no perspective, so at 1600 px the frames still read as a
  set rather than a place. The saving grace is that the contact sheet is a study of light, and light
  is what the frames actually show.
- **Monotony is the risk and the point.** Twenty-four near-identical framings will read as boring
  to anyone who wants twenty-four pictures. The tonal ladder is the only thing that makes the sheet
  a sequence; if the ladder were any flatter the piece would collapse. I believe it is steep enough,
  but it is close.
- **The red tick is almost too quiet.** It is 2.8×12 px of red at the top-left of the black frame —
  findable only if you look. That is deliberate (the one human mark on a machine-printed sheet) but
  at 400 px it is a speck, and a viewer may read it as a printing fault rather than a mark.
- **The edge markings are plausible rather than authentic.** They are graphics of film-edge
  perforations and frame ticks, not a real 35 mm layout; the sheet is a print, not a negative.
- What works: the grid holds — the gutters, the black frame borders and the film strips make a
  lattice that survives the thumbnail; the noon row genuinely burns and the night row genuinely
  goes out; the one black frame stops the eye faster than any of the twenty-three photographs.
